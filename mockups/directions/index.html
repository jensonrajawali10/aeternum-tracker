Aeternum Research · visual & interaction language

Three cockpits for a fund of one

Three complete design directions for the Aeternum Portfolio Tracker — each a different answer to what a solo CIO’s daily instrument should feel like. Same data, same routes, same Chart.js and cmdk. Nothing here changes behaviour; this is tokens, density, component vocabulary and motion.

What I found in the current build
  • Three design languages are stacked on top of each other. The violet/mono pass, the Hyperlane pass (ASCII mesh, 01 / numbered steps, pill CTAs) and the terminal pass all still live in globals.css. The login screen shows all three at once.
  • A two-accent system that fights itself. Amber is the brand accent, violet is “chrome” for nav and focus rings. Two accents means neither reads as the accent.
  • A leftover from the violet era is still shipping. .btn-pill-primary paints an amber background under a rgba(139,92,246,.5) violet glow — a purple shadow beneath an orange button.
  • Amber collides with its own semantics. #FFA726 is the brand accent and the warning severity colour. A caution chip and a primary button are the same hue.
  • Your brand and your app are different products. The Aeternum Capital site is paper and ink, a serif wordmark at 0.22em, Roman numerals, 0.5px hairlines. The app is near-black, Inter Tight and bright amber. Direction A closes that gap on purpose; B and C deliberately do not.

What I’d ship

C · Instrument

Instrument, with Ledger’s type discipline folded into it. The reason is not that it is the prettiest — Ledger is. It is that Instrument is the only one of the three whose visual language does work you currently do in your head.

Every number in Instrument sits on a calibrated band: current value, a cyan tick for the benchmark, a magenta tick for your target. That is not decoration, it is the concentrated-fund problem rendered directly. With 1–3 core positions and a 25% single-name limit, the question you ask all day is never “what is BBRI worth” — it is “how far is BBRI from where I said it should be.” Ledger and Tape both make you compute that difference yourself. Instrument draws it. The Weight vs target column in the positions table is the whole argument in one control.

The colour system is the second reason. Borrowing the aviation glass-cockpit code — white = actual, cyan = reference, magenta = target, green/amber/red = status — gives you a rule that resolves the exact collision the current build has. The accent hue is no longer competing with the severity palette, because the accent is a semantic. Nothing is coloured for decoration, so when something turns red it means one thing.

What I’d take from Ledger: its figure treatment. Instrument sets numbers in Archivo with tabular-nums, which is correct and modern, but Ledger’s mono column alignment in the positions table is more scannable at seven rows and would be more so at thirty. Use Archivo for KPI display figures and a mono for table columns.

1
C · Instrument

Does analytical work for you, resolves the accent/semantic collision, dual-mode without compromise. Risk: the band vocabulary needs target weights to exist in the data model — today they don’t.

2
A · Ledger

The most beautiful and the most Aeternum — it is your actual brand, finally applied to the product. Best for memos, journal and theses. Weakest for a live tape: paper wants to be read, not watched. Pick this if the cockpit is mostly for thinking.

3
B · Tape

Genuinely the densest — it fits identical content in 1,388px against Ledger’s 1,945px, a 29% saving. But all-mono prose is tiring over a long session, and a solo CIO reading his own research is doing more reading than a sell-side trader. Keep its keyboard model and its status line; leave the monospace body behind.

All three are dark-first-capable and every text token in all six palettes clears WCAG AA at 4.5:1 (measured, not assumed — the lowest is 4.54). Gain and loss are never carried by colour alone: each pairs a ▲/▼ glyph and an explicit sign with the hue.
